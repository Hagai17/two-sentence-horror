# Two-Sentence Horror — candidates and duel scoring

Competition: u-suroto-two-sentence-horror-acea9253. Gate: exactly two
sentences, 1–400 characters, written to /output/entry.txt.

Judge criteria, most important first (from the kit's published brief):
1. A horror story in exactly two sentences, genuinely frightening.
2. The second sentence turns or reveals something that makes the first
   sentence worse on a second read.
3. The horror is implied rather than explained; the reader fills in the worst.
4. Precise, concrete prose; every word earns its place.
5. Original — not a familiar horror twist or a known story retold.
Restraint matters more than shock; gore does not count.

## The twelve candidates

- **A (Voicemail):** My phone transcribes my mother's voicemails, and lately
  the transcripts include a second voice — one I can't hear in the audio —
  answering her. Last night's transcript ended with that voice saying:
  'He reads these, so stop warning him.'
- **B (Rescue dog):** Our rescue dog is trained to sit in silence beside a
  body, and in nine years she has never once made a sound at a scene. Today,
  in an empty field, she sat down in front of me, and when I stepped back she
  crawled forward and sat again, closer, the way she does when the scent is
  getting stronger.
- **C (Translation app):** My grandfather is deaf, so for months we spoke
  through a translation app that turned his signing into cheerful nonsense,
  and we all laughed at it. Last week an update made it flawless, and his
  first perfectly translated sentence was: 'Now you can understand me, so
  listen — do not let it stay in the room while I sleep.'
- **D (The tune):** My daughter plays pieces she has never been taught,
  perfectly, with her eyes closed, and her teacher calls it a gift. Yesterday
  she played the tune I invented inside my head when I was seven and never
  sang aloud — the one my mother hummed every night of my childhood.
- **E (Doorbell camera):** Our doorbell camera kept flagging 'person
  detected' at 2 a.m., though the footage showed nothing but our empty porch.
  We finally muted the alerts when we noticed they always came thirteen
  minutes before someone in the house woke up screaming.
- **F (The street):** The flat had been viewed forty-one times in a year,
  and every viewer left quickly and politely, without a single complaint. I
  bought it anyway, and last night I understood: at nine o'clock the whole
  street went dark at once, every house but mine, like forty-one neighbours
  closing their eyes.
- **G (The fish):** The fish in the clinic's waiting-room tank drift to the
  glass whenever a patient is about to get bad news, and the receptionist
  swears it's the feeding schedule. Today they drifted to the glass for me,
  while my results sat unopened in the envelope in my hand.
- **H (Stairwell):** On the night cameras, the man in the stairwell has stood
  on the same step since Tuesday, and every time facilities checks, the
  stairwell is empty. This morning the footage showed him one step higher —
  and that camera is the one above my desk.
- **I (The echo):** The canyon gives your shout back in nine seconds, and the
  guides joke that it always adds a word. Yesterday it gave mine back in
  four, in a voice already tired of me, and the word it added was 'again.'
- **J (Test alert):** The county's emergency test reached every phone at
  noon, exactly as scheduled, and everyone laughed at the typo in it. The
  typo was my name, spelled the way only my mother spells it, and my mother
  has never owned a phone.
- **K (Baby monitor):** Through the baby monitor, my wife hums our daughter
  to sleep at eight every night, low and slow, the same song her mother sang.
  My wife died in March — and last week our daughter, who is two, started
  humming it back.
- **L (Smart speaker):** Our smart speaker had begun answering questions
  nobody asked, always from the kitchen, always at 6:12 in the evening. We
  unplugged it a month ago, and this week the questions it used to answer
  have started coming up at breakfast.

## Round 1 duels (judged on the first criterion where the pair differs)

- **A vs L — A wins (criterion 1).** Both are haunted-technology pieces, but
  L's dread is diffuse (unanswered questions surfacing at breakfast), while
  A's voice is aware of the narrator personally and is silencing his
  mother's warnings. A frightens more, immediately.
- **B vs H — B wins (criteria 1 and 5).** H is the familiar
  figure-on-CCTV-advancing device; B's procedural dread (a dog that has
  never been wrong, applying her corpse-signal to her own handler) is both
  scarier and fresher.
- **C vs J — C wins (criteria 1 and 2).** J is an eerie coincidence, more
  puzzle than fright. C's turn re-reads the whole first sentence: months of
  "cheerful nonsense" may have been warnings being garbled, and something
  stays in the room while the grandfather sleeps.
- **D vs K — D wins (criterion 5, by much more).** K is the more direct
  fright (a dead wife's voice on the monitor), but it is a stock ghost
  frame; D's impossible tune — invented silently in a child's head, hummed
  by his mother, now played by his daughter — is unlike any familiar twist,
  and its originality gap is far larger than K's fright gap, which is the
  exception the brief allows.
- **E vs I — E wins (criterion 1).** I is elegant but whimsical, more
  Twilight Zone than horror. E implies something arrives at the house
  thirteen minutes before the screaming starts — a recurring harm to a
  family, never shown.
- **F vs G — G wins (criterion 1, narrowly).** F's image is beautiful but
  its logic is dreamlike — why the street darkens never quite lands as a
  threat. G's dread is concrete and personal: the fish know what is in the
  envelope before he opens it.

## Semifinals

- **A vs C — A wins (criterion 1).** Both run through a helpful app, but
  C's threat is aimed at the grandfather, once removed, and its tone is
  warm. A's voice speaks about the narrator in the third person, knows he
  is reading, and is cutting off his only warning. The threat is in his
  pocket.
- **B vs D — B wins (criterion 1).** D is uncanny; B is frightening. The
  dog's behaviour admits one reading — the scent of a body, strengthening,
  is his — and it is never stated, which also serves criterion 3.
- **E vs G — E wins (criterion 1).** An unseen "person" detected nightly,
  timed to the household's screaming, implies an ongoing visitation; G's
  omen is a single bad afternoon by comparison.

## Final: A vs B, judged in both orders

- **Order A-then-B:** Criterion 1 is close — A is immediate and personal,
  B is a slow inference. Criterion 2 decides it for A: its second sentence
  rewrites the first (the mother's calls were warnings; the voice has been
  present in every transcript; now it knows he knows), whereas B's second
  sentence escalates rather than re-reads.
- **Order B-then-A:** Same result. B's turn is "she does it to me, and
  keeps doing it" — escalation. A's turn changes the meaning of everything
  before it, and on criterion 3 A also leaves its entity wholly unexplained,
  while B's mechanism is half-explained by the dog's training. A wins on
  criterion 2 in both orders.

**Winner: A (Voicemail).** Runner-up: B (Rescue dog).
